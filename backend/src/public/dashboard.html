A practical walkthrough - what to actually click, in order, to get a stream on your website.

  1. 1

    Create a channel

    Click "+ Create channel" on the Channels page and give it a name - the room, service, or camera it's for. Each channel gets its own stream key, settings, and outputs, entirely separate from any others.

  2. Connect your encoder

    Copy the channel's RTMP URL and Stream key into OBS, vMix, or whatever you're streaming from, then press "Start Streaming" there. There's no separate "go live" button here - the channel goes live automatically the moment your encoder connects.

    2
  3. 3

    Put the player on your website

    With "Your Website" switched on, copy the embed code shown and paste it into your site's HTML. It can sit there permanently, live or not - it only starts downloading video once a visitor actually presses play.

  4. Relay to other platforms too

    Click "+ Add output" to also send the same stream to a custom RTMP address (Facebook and most other platforms work this way too - just paste in their RTMP URL and stream key), or YouTube via its own connect button, or generate a Public Link viewers can open directly. Turn any of them on or off whenever you like, independently of the others.

    4
  5. 5

    Turn on recording if you want a copy saved

    Flip the "Record streams" switch before you go live. Once you stop, the recording finishes processing and appears on the Recordings page - usually within 5-30 minutes - ready to play back or download. It's never public, admin-only.

  6. Add cover and thumbnail images

    On a channel's page, upload an offline cover (shown before you're live) and a live thumbnail (shown once you're live, before someone presses play). Upload as many as you like to either gallery, then click one to make it the active image.

    6
  7. 7

    Check who's watching

    The Stats page shows anonymised, aggregate viewer numbers per channel - roughly where and how many, filterable by date and exportable as CSV. No accounts, no personal data, ever.

  8. Rename or delete a channel any time

    Both live on the channel's own page. Deleting one is permanent - it revokes the stream key and switches off every output for that channel, so double-check before confirming.

    8

The mechanics behind the site - what actually happens on the server, end to end.

  1. 1

    Your encoder pushes video in over RTMP

    OBS, vMix, or whatever you're using sends a live video feed to this server using RTMP, a streaming protocol built for exactly this. Your channel's Stream key is checked on connection - it's the only thing standing between "this is really you" and anyone else, so it doubles as your password for that channel.

  2. The server converts it to HLS in real time

    The incoming feed is immediately re-packaged into HLS - a few seconds of video at a time, written out as small files plus a constantly-updated playlist pointing at the latest ones. It's the same technique YouTube, Netflix, and most of the web use, because it plays over plain HTTPS in any browser without a video plugin.

    2
  3. 3

    Viewers never touch the ingest server directly

    The RTMP server itself isn't reachable from outside at all - the backend fetches those HLS files internally and serves them to viewers itself, over the same domain as everything else. There's nothing for a viewer's browser to connect to except this one website.

  4. The embed player checks in, then pulls video on demand

    The small player embedded on your website polls the backend every few seconds asking "is this channel live yet?" - it only starts actually downloading video once someone presses play, not the moment the page loads, so an embedded-but-unwatched player costs nothing in bandwidth.

    4
  5. 5

    Relaying is a live copy, made server-side

    If an output is turned on, the server spins up a separate process that reads the same incoming feed and forwards an exact copy of it, unmodified, to that destination (a custom RTMP address, or YouTube via its API) for as long as you're live. It's a straight copy, not a re-encode, so it costs the server very little CPU and your encoder never knows or cares how many places it's going to.

  6. Recording writes to local disk first, then uploads and self-deletes

    With recording on, the same incoming feed is also copied to a raw file on the server's own disk as it arrives. The moment you stop, that file is converted into a normal, seekable video file, uploaded to Cloudflare's cloud storage, and then deleted from the server itself - nothing from a finished recording is ever left sitting on the droplet.

    6
  7. 7

    Stats come from the player quietly pinging back

    While someone is actually watching, their player periodically pings the backend, which logs a rough, IP-based location and device type against that channel - never anything that identifies who they are, since nobody needs an account to watch. That's the raw material behind the Stats page.

  8. Everything's kept on one small server, by design

    Channel settings, outputs, and admin logins all live in a lightweight data file on the same server, not a separate database - there's no infrastructure to manage beyond this one machine, sized for one organisation's traffic rather than a large public platform.

    8
